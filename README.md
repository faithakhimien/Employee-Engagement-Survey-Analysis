## Introduction:

Employee engagement is one of the most critical drivers of organizational performance, productivity, and retention, especially in public sector institutions where workforce morale directly impacts community service quality.

This project analyzes actual employee engagement survey responses collected voluntarily by Pierce County, a government organization in Washington, covering 21 departments and multiple job role levels. The dataset contains over 14,000 records across 10 fields, capturing employee sentiment across 10 key engagement topics.


## Key Metrics:

1. Most Agreed & Disagreed Topics
2. Patterns by Department and Role
3. Engagement Gap

### Analysis:

### Most Agreed & Disagreed Topics
Most Agreed (Highest Scores):
- Role Clarity (3.5) - Employees broadly understand what is expected of them. This is the top-performing topic across the survey, suggesting Pierce County has done well in communicating job responsibilities.
- Supervisor Care (3.3) - Employees feel their supervisors show genuine interest in their wellbeing, indicating a relatively healthy management culture.
- Best Self at Work (3.1) and Inclusion & Diversity (3.1) - Both score at the midpoint, suggesting neutral-to-positive feelings with room for growth.

Most Disagreed (Lowest Scores):
- Best Friend at Work (2.6) - This is the single lowest-scoring topic. It measures social belonging and peer connection, a known predictor of engagement and retention. Low scores here suggest employees feel socially isolated in their work environment.
- Recognition & Praise (2.8) - Employees do not feel their contributions are being adequately acknowledged, which is a direct driver of disengagement and turnover.
- Accountability (2.9) - Scores suggest employees may feel a lack of consistent standards or follow-through on commitments across teams.

### Patterns by Department and Role
Department Patterns:
- Family Justice Center (3.6) and Emergency Management (3.5) lead the organization, suggesting strong leadership and team culture in high-stakes service environments.
- Sheriff’s Department (2.5) is the clear outlier at the bottom, scoring a full point below the leader. Given the stressful, hierarchical nature of law enforcement culture, this gap likely reflects issues with recognition, peer connection, and psychological safety.
- Mid-scoring departments (3.1–3.2) represent the largest cluster, indicating that moderate disengagement is widespread, not isolated.
  
Role Patterns:
A clear top-down score gradient exists - Directors (3.4) → Managers (3.3) → Supervisors (3.2) → General Staff/Staff (3.0). The further down the hierarchy, the lower the engagement. Senior roles tend to come with more autonomy, visibility, and purpose, all of which naturally boost engagement.

### Engagement Gap
The gap between Director-level (3.4) and Staff-level (3.0) scores, combined with the low Recognition & Praise scores (2.8) and Best Friend at Work scores (2.6), creates a compounding problem. Frontline employees who feel unseen, unappreciated, and socially disconnected are more likely to mentally check out or walk out entirely.

## Conclusion and Recommendations:

Pierce County employees largely know what is expected of them and feel supported by their supervisors, which is a solid foundation to build on. That said, the data points to real gaps in how connected, recognized, and accountable employees feel day to day. These are not minor issues. The steady drop in scores from Director level down to General Staff, and the Sheriff’s Department sitting at the bottom with a 2.5, suggest that some parts of the organization need more than a policy update to turn things around.


The most immediate priority is making employees feel seen. Recognition and Praise scored just 2.8, and that is the kind of gap that quietly drives good people out the door. A structured recognition program across all departments would go a long way. 

The Sheriff’s Department needs direct attention, starting with honest listening sessions and a clear action plan that leadership is held to. 

On the social side, employees who feel no sense of connection with their colleagues are less likely to stay engaged, so mentorship pairings and cross-department activities are worth investing in. Managers also need support in modeling accountability, because when follow-through is inconsistent at the top, it filters down. For frontline staff specifically, a visible path for career growth would signal that the organization values them beyond their current role.

Finally, running a follow-up survey in six months would show employees that this was not just a data collection exercise but a genuine commitment to change.
